# Why 95% of AI Pilots Return Nothing

**Subtitle:** The tools work in the demo. The money never shows up. That gap is a choice problem, not a technology problem.

---

MIT looked at hundreds of corporate AI efforts last year and found that 95% showed no measurable return. Zero impact on profit or loss. Companies spent the money, ran the pilot, saw the demo work, and the number at the bottom of the page did not move.

The failure is almost never the model.

## Start with the honest part

The 95% figure is contested, and it should be. It came from the MIT NANDA "State of AI in Business 2025" report, and the sample underneath it is thin: 52 interviews, 153 surveys, a few hundred deployments. That is a read on the market, not an audit of anyone's books. Treat it as directional, not gospel.

I lead with the caveat on purpose. If a number is doing real work in an argument, you should know how much weight it can hold. This one points in a clear direction. It cannot tell you the exact size of the problem.

## The direction holds up everywhere else

Look past the one study and the pattern repeats.

PwC's 2026 Global CEO Survey found that 56% of chief executives saw neither more revenue nor lower costs from AI. The same survey put CFO and CEO misalignment on AI at 65%, so the people writing the checks and the people reading the results are not even looking at the same scoreboard.

Gartner predicted that at least 30% of generative AI projects would be abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025. S&P found that 42% of companies abandoned most of their AI projects in 2025.

Different researchers, different methods, same shape. The tools work in the demo. The return does not arrive.

## If it is not the technology, what is it

The models are good enough. That is the uncomfortable part for anyone hoping the next release fixes this. When the software runs in the pilot and the money still does not show up, the problem sits upstream of the technology, in the choices made before anyone wrote a prompt.

The MIT work is useful here because it also found what worked, not only what failed.

Partnered builds succeeded about twice as often as in-house ones. Companies that brought in people who had done it before hit roughly a 67% success rate. Companies that built alone hit about 33%. Buying the model was never the hard part. Knowing where to point it was.

The quiet back-office automation returned more than the flashy sales and marketing bets that most budgets chase. The projects that photograph well for a board deck are not the projects that pay. The boring ones pay.

Put those together and the pattern is about choices. Which use case you picked. Whether you defined success before you started. Whether anyone can look at the result and say plainly whether it worked.

## The test that separates a pilot from an experiment

Before leaders fund anything, I give them one filter.

Write one sentence that says what this pilot has to prove and by when. If you cannot write that sentence, you do not have a pilot. You have a science experiment on the company card.

A real pilot sounds like this: "By the end of Q3, this cuts invoice processing time in half without adding headcount." You can grade that. It passed or it did not. A science experiment sounds like this: "We are exploring how AI can transform our operations." Nobody can grade that, which means it can run forever, spend real money, and never be wrong, because it was never testing anything.

Most failed pilots are the second kind wearing the clothes of the first.

## What this means if you run a company

The next AI project you fund will not fail because the model is not smart enough. It will fail because the use case was chosen for how it looked, not what it returned. Because no one wrote down what success meant. Because you built alone when you should have brought in help. Because the result was never something anyone could grade.

Those are all decisions you make before the work starts, which means they are the decisions you can fix.

Before you approve the next one, write the one sentence: what must this prove, and by when. If you cannot write it, that is your first problem to solve, and it is a cheaper one to solve now than after the budget is gone.
