Last year MIT put hundreds of corporate AI initiatives under a microscope and the verdict was 95% returned nothing. Not a dent in the profit and loss statement. The company would put up the cash, run the pilot, watch the demo do its thing, and the bottom line would not budge.

Hardly ever is it the model at fault.

Then again, one could argue with that 95% number. It is drawn from the sample in the MIT NANDA “State of AI in Business 2025” report which is on the thin side: 153 surveys, 52 interviews, a few hundred deployments. Call it a read on the market, not an audit of the books. I like to be clear about how much weight a figure can carry in an argument; this one is directional, not gospel.

But you will see the pattern if you look beyond any single study. PwC’s 2026 Global CEO Survey has 56% of chief executives reporting no gain in revenue or cost savings from AI. Their survey also found a 65% misalignment between the CFO and CEO on AI, meaning the ones signing off on the checks and the ones looking at the results are not even using the same scoreboard. Gartner expects 30% of generative AI work to be shelved after proof of concept by year end. S&P says 42% of firms have already walked away from most of their AI projects in 2025.

Different methods, different researchers, but the outcome is the same. The tool performs in the demo, the return does not materialise. And for those waiting for the next release to be the answer, the models are quite good enough. When the numbers do not show up despite a successful pilot, the issue is upstream of the technology, in the decisions made before a prompt was ever written.

MIT’s research is instructive because they noted what works as well as what does not. In-house builds only had a 33% success rate. But pair up with someone who has been there and done that and you are looking at roughly 67%. You do not have trouble buying the model; you have trouble knowing where to aim it.

The quiet automation in the back office puts more money on the table than the sales and marketing stunts most budgets are drawn to. What looks good on a board deck is not what pays.

So when I am talking with leaders about funding something, I offer a simple filter. Put it in one sentence: what does this pilot have to prove and when? If you cannot write that, you do not have a pilot. You have a science experiment running on the company card.

“By Q3 we will have halved invoice processing time with no new headcount.” That is a real pilot. You can grade it. “We are exploring how AI can transform our operations” is a science experiment. It can go on forever, cost you real money and never be wrong since it is not testing anything. Most of the pilots that fail are the latter in disguise.

Your next project is not going to come undone because the model lacks smarts. It will be because you chose a use case for its appearance, built it alone when you should have sought help, or left the definition of success unwritten. Those are all things you decide before the work begins and therefore things you can put right.

Make sure you can write the one sentence on what must be proved before you give the go ahead. It is a cheaper problem to solve now than once the budget is spent.

I have seen this pattern with every client. If you have had an AI pilot that went nowhere, I would wager the setup was the issue, not the tools. Tell me about it in the comments.
