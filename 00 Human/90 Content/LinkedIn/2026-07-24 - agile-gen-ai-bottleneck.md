# LinkedIn Article - Agile and the moved bottleneck

## Angle
> Agile's ceremonies exist because code was the slow, expensive, unpredictable part of building software. Gen-AI made writing code cheap, so the bottleneck moved to knowing what to build, verifying output, and maintaining it. Teams running the old ceremonies feel a strange gap: work is easier but delivery does not speed up. Speaks to Joe CEO who adopted AI tools and cannot understand why throughput did not change.

## Body

For most of my twenty years building software, the slowest part of any project was writing the code.

That is the fact Agile was built around. Scrum, sprints, story points, the daily standup. Every one of those practices exists because code was expensive, slow, and hard to predict. You broke work into two-week batches because you could not see far ahead. You estimated in points because you were guessing at effort. You demoed every sprint because building the wrong thing for three months was a disaster you could not afford.

The methodology fit the constraint. The constraint has moved.

An engineer working with a capable AI model now produces a working feature in hours that used to take days. I watch it happen on client projects every week. The typing, the boilerplate, the first draft of a function. The part that used to eat the calendar is close to free.

The bottleneck is somewhere else now, and most teams are still running the ceremonies built for the old one.

### Where the time actually goes

When code gets cheap to write, three things become the expensive parts.

The first is knowing what to build. A model will generate whatever you ask for. It has no opinion on whether the feature matters, whether the requirement is right, or whether the customer will use it. Point it at a bad idea and it builds the bad idea faster than ever.

The second is verifying the output. AI writes code that looks correct, reads well, and sometimes does the wrong thing in a way that takes a senior engineer to catch. Reviewing that work, testing it, and understanding it well enough to own it is now the real labor.

The third is integrating and maintaining what you shipped. Code you generated in an afternoon still has to live in your system for years. Someone has to keep it running, secure it, and change it later.

None of those three get easier from a story-point estimate.

### What an updated process looks like

I am not arguing to throw out Agile. The instincts behind it were right: small batches, fast feedback, working software over documentation. Those hold up. The ceremonies need to point at the new bottleneck instead of the old one.

Estimation should move from how long a thing takes to build toward how sure you are it is the right thing to build. That is where the risk lives now. A team spending its planning time arguing about points is measuring the cheap part.

Review has to become a first-class activity with real time budgeted for it, not a box checked at the end. When one engineer can generate a week of code in a day, the team's capacity to review and understand that code is the true limit on how fast you can safely ship.

The loop should get shorter, not longer. Two-week sprints made sense when two weeks was the smallest unit of meaningful progress. When a validated change can go from idea to production in two days, a two-week batch is holding finished work hostage.

The engineer's job changes too. Less time producing first drafts. More time on judgment: architecture, tradeoffs, catching the subtle failure, deciding what not to build. The people who thrive are the ones who were already strong at those things. AI raised the value of judgment and lowered the value of typing.

### What this means if you run a company

If your teams adopted AI tools but kept every ceremony exactly as it was, you are getting a fraction of the return. The tool got faster. The process around it did not. You will feel it as a strange gap. Engineers say the work is easier, but delivery does not speed up the way you expected. The drag is in the process, not the people.

Ask your engineering leaders one question this week. Now that writing code is no longer the slow part, what is? Your answer decides whether your process still fits your work.
